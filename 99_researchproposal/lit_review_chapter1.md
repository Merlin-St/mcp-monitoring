## I. SEMINAL THEORETICAL WORKS

### 1. Menard, C. (2004). "The Economics of Hybrid Organizations"
**Citation**: *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics*, 160(3): 345-376.

**Core Contribution**: Ménard develops a framework for understanding organizational forms that lie between pure markets and pure hierarchies. Hybrid organizations maintain legal control over strategic property rights (remaining distinct residual claimants) while pooling resources and related decision rights.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Discrete Alignment Principle**: Hybrid organizations are chosen to align governance structures with exchange attributes to minimize transaction costs (building on Williamson 1991)
- **Regulating Devices**: Hybrids contain "authorities" (distinct from hierarchies) as core architectural elements
- **Fundamental Factors**: Transaction costs, asset specificity, and uncertainty determine optimal governance form

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your work directly adapts this framework to auditing regimes, asking which fundamental factors (risk externality scale, verification costs, information sensitivity, risk uncertainty, market concentration) determine whether public or private bodies should conduct AI evaluations.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited and central to theoretical framework. Good engagement.

### 2. Williamson, O. E. (1979, 2008). Transaction Cost Economics
**Citations**:
- (1979) "Transaction-Cost Economics: The Governance of Contractual Relations." *Journal of Law and Economics*, 22(2): 233-261.
- (2008) "Outsourcing: Transaction Cost Economics and Supply Chain Management." *Journal of Supply Chain Management*, 44(2): 5-16.

**Core Contribution**: Transaction cost economics (TCE) provides the foundational logic for understanding organizational boundaries and governance choices. TCE focuses on economizing transaction costs through alignment of governance structures with transaction characteristics.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Asset Specificity**: Degree to which investments have significantly lower value in next-best alternative use
- **Bounded Rationality**: Limits on capacity to receive, store, retrieve, and process information without error
- **Opportunism**: Self-interest seeking with guile
- **Frequency of Exchange**: How often transactions occur
- **Uncertainty**: Unpredictability in transaction environment

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your framework adapts Williamson's three key dimensions (transaction costs, asset specificity, uncertainty) to the auditing context. You translate asset specificity into "information sensitivity" and "skill specificity," which is methodologically sound.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited and appropriately applied. Your translation of TCE to auditing context is theoretically coherent.

---

### 3. Quélin, B. V., Kivleniece, I., & Lazzarini, S. (2017). "Public-Private Collaboration, Hybridity and Social Value"
**Citation**: *Journal of Management*, 43(5): 1331-1350.

**Core Contribution**: Extends hybrid governance theory to explicitly address public-private collaboration, introducing the concept of "hybridity" as a spectrum rather than discrete categories. Emphasizes how hybrid arrangements can create social value beyond economic efficiency.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Hybridity Spectrum**: Continuous range of contractual relations between hierarchical and market-driven oversight
- **Public Value Creation**: Hybrid arrangements can address market failures while avoiding government failures
- **Triple Embeddedness**: Hybrids are embedded in institutional, network, and organizational contexts

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your two-step decision logic for determining auditor responsibilities reflects this spectrum thinking. You acknowledge that oversight regimes exist along a continuum of hybridity rather than as binary public/private choices.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Quélin et al. 2019 version). Good theoretical grounding.

### 4. Aven, T. & Renn, O. (2009, 2018). Risk Governance Frameworks
**Citations**:
- (2009) "On Risk Defined as an Event Where the Outcome is Uncertain." *Journal of Risk Research*, 12(1): 1-11.
- (2010) *Risk Management and Governance: Concepts, Guidelines and Applications*. Springer.
- (2011) "Coping with Complexity, Uncertainty and Ambiguity in Risk Governance: A Synthesis." *Ambio*, 40(2): 231-246.

**Core Contribution**: Develops comprehensive framework for risk governance that distinguishes between complexity, scientific uncertainty, and sociopolitical ambiguity. Argues for hybrid strategies that integrate risk-informed, precautionary, and discursive approaches.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Risk Definition**: "Uncertainty about and severity of consequences of an activity with respect to something humans value"
- **Complexity**: Difficulty identifying and quantifying causal links between multiple potential candidates and adverse effects
- **Uncertainty**: Limitedness or absence of scientific knowledge (data, information)
- **Ambiguity**: Plurality of legitimate viewpoints for evaluating outcomes and tolerability
- **Hybrid Strategies**: Emphasize systemic understanding and resilience for managing complexity, uncertainty, ambiguity

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your "risk uncertainty" factor directly maps to Aven & Renn's uncertainty dimension. Advanced AI fits their characterization of "uncertain risks" with potential for extreme consequences and large uncertainties about nature/extent of consequences. Your emphasis on "knowledge aspects" for risk management strategy aligns with their framework.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited and well-applied. Your quantification of risk uncertainty via ISO standards (length & share under development) is creative operationalization.

### 5. Rosa, E. A. (1998, 2003, 2010). Risk Definition and Classification
**Citations**:
- (1998) "Metatheoretical Foundations for Post-Normal Risk." *Journal of Risk Research*, 1(1): 15-44.
- (2003) "The Logical Structure of the Social Amplification of Risk Framework." In *The Social Amplification of Risk*.

**Core Contribution**: Provides widely-adopted definition of risk in social sciences: "A situation or event where something of human value is at stake and where outcome is uncertain." Develops classification of risk problem classes.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Three Risk Problem Classes**: Simple, uncertain, and complex
- **Uncertain Risks**: Limited understanding of underlying phenomena, potential for extreme consequences, large uncertainties about nature/extent
- **Risk Management Strategies**: For uncertain risks, focus on "knowledge aspects"

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your identification of advanced AI as predominantly "uncertain" (not simple or complex) follows Rosa's classification. This justifies your focus on information collection mechanisms as primary governance strategy.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Rosa, Renn & McCright 2015). Appropriate application to AI risk characterization.

## II. REGULATORY CAPTURE & INFORMATION ASYMMETRIES

### 6. Dal Bó, E. (2006). "Regulatory Capture: A Review"
**Citation**: *Oxford Review of Economic Policy*, 22(2): 203-225.

**Core Contribution**: Comprehensive review of regulatory capture theory, from Stigler-Peltzman approach through modern principal-agent models under asymmetric information. Examines how industry influence shapes regulatory outcomes.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Classic Capture**: Industry influence leads to anti-competitive conditions protecting incumbents
- **Information Asymmetries**: Regulators lack insider information, making it difficult to discern whether information is colored by private interests
- **Regulatory Discretion**: Hierarchical agency models explain how capture occurs through information control

**Recent Extension (October 2024 arXiv)**: Paper on "How Do AI Companies Fine-Tune Policy? Examining Regulatory Capture in AI Governance" adapts Dal Bó's definitions to AI context. Finds that AI industry actors have large information asymmetries over government and can shape policy narratives by strategically controlling/releasing information.

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your "information sensitivity" factor captures asset specificity concerns that enable capture. Your framework predicts public evaluation when information sensitivity is high, directly addressing capture risks.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Dal Bó 2006). Strong connection to Chapter 1 argument about why public bodies should conduct evaluations under high information sensitivity.

---

### 7. Stigler, G. J. (1971). "The Theory of Economic Regulation"
**Citation**: *The Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science*, 2(1): 3-21.

**Core Contribution**: Foundational work arguing that regulation is acquired by industry and designed/operated primarily for its benefit. Introduces economic theory of regulation focusing on how regulatory costs/benefits distribute across stakeholders.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Industry Capture of Regulation**: Industries seek regulation that creates barriers to entry
- **Information Advantages**: Specialized knowledge creates power asymmetries
- **Regulator Incentives**: Regulators may align with industry due to career incentives (revolving door)

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your market concentration factor (Herfindahl Index) captures Stigler's insight about industry structure affecting capture risk. High concentration = greater ability to coordinate lobbying and information control.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Stigler 1971). Appropriately applied.

---

### 8. Carpenter, D. & Moss, D. A. (Eds.). (2013). *Preventing Regulatory Capture: Special Interest Influence and How to Limit It*
**Citation**: Cambridge University Press.

**Core Contribution**: Modern examination of regulatory capture mechanisms and potential solutions. Emphasizes role of transparency, expertise development, and institutional design in preventing capture.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Capture Prevention**: Requires sufficiently high oversight on auditors
- **Expertise Asymmetries**: Sectors requiring specialized knowledge (like high-tech) experience more capture
- **Institutional Safeguards**: Transparency requirements, expert advisory panels, public participation

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your emphasis on "oversight on auditors is sufficiently high" directly echoes Carpenter & Moss. Your framework's prediction that public bodies should evaluate AI under high uncertainty/sensitivity addresses their capture prevention strategies.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Carpenter & Moss 2013). Good engagement.

## III. RISK GOVERNANCE & ADAPTIVE REGULATION

### 9. Black, J. (2005). "The Emergence of Risk-Based Regulation and the New Public Risk Management in the United Kingdom"
**Citation**: *Public Law*, Autumn 2005: 512-548.

**Core Contribution**: Analyzes shift toward risk-based regulation emphasizing that effective risk regulation requires up-to-date information about risks and risk-factors of regulated subjects.

**Key Concepts**:
- **Risk-Based Regulation**: Regulatory intensity proportional to risk assessment
- **Information Requirements**: Regulators require continuous information about risks and risk-factors
- **Adaptive Regulation**: Must evolve as understanding of risks changes

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Your opening statement "For risk-based regulation, regulators require information about the risks and risk-factors" directly cites Black's framework. Your entire project addresses how to organize information collection for risk-based AI regulation.

**Assessment**: ✓ Cited (Black 2005). Central to your motivation for information collection focus.

**Citation**: *Policy and Society*, 40(2): 178-193.

**Key Concepts**:

- **National Strategy Variation**: Different institutional contexts lead to different governance choices

## IV. AI-SPECIFIC AUDITING & EVALUATION LITERATURE (2024-2025)

### 11. Farley, K. & Lansang, J. (2025). "AI Auditing: First Steps Towards the Effective Regulation of Artificial Intelligence"
**Citation**: *Harvard Journal of Law & Technology*, 2(13): publication 2.13.2025.

**Core Contribution**: Most comprehensive recent analysis of AI auditing frameworks, identifying the biggest obstacle as lack of mature framework detailing AI subprocesses and baseline controls.

**Key Findings (2024-2025)**:
- **Principles-to-Practice Gap**: Especially pronounced for AI ethics and safety auditing
- **Practitioner Challenges**: Difficulty independently accessing high-quality data about system behavior, applying consistent standards, ensuring audit integrity, involving stakeholders, collaborating across disciplines
- **Maturity Problem**: Absence of baseline controls renders audit results inconsistent and unreliable

**Recent Developments**:
- Internal auditing and red-teaming more common prior to frontier model release
- Discussion of mandatory external and independent auditing to address principles-to-practice gap

**Relevance to Chapter 1**: Directly validates your research motivation. Your Chapter 1 published findings on when public bodies should conduct evaluations addresses exactly these practitioner challenges and principles-to-practice gaps.

**Assessment**: ⚠️ NOT cited - This is major recent work (Feb 2025) that should be cited in transfer document to show engagement with latest AI auditing literature.
