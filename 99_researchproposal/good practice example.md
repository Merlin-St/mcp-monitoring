Abstract:
Chameleon Industries in Sectoral Green Transitions
Jonas Fischer
May 13, 2024
Word count: 157
Emissions from various economic sectors must be eliminated to prevent catastrophic
global warming. However, different sectors have been and are predicted to be on widely
differing emissions trajectories. My doctoral research project will explain this sectoral
variation. Building on business power literature, I bring forward a theory of chameleon
industries. Actors in these industries can change color from brown to green due to their
low asset specificity. Such a color change shifts the sectoral power equilibrium between the
brown (opposing climate policy) and green (supporting climate policy) business coalition
towards the latter, which allows policymakers to pass more ambitious climate policies
that reduce sectoral emissions. I will test my argument in four case studies on the role
of business in European Union policymaking in the electricity, road transport, aviation,
and agriculture sectors. The case studies will follow a process-tracing methodology, and
within-case evidence will be uncovered through elite interviews with executives, lobbyists,
and policymakers.

1 Introduction
Fundamental renegotiations in production and consumption are necessary to keep climate
change within natural tipping points. Climate change mitigation policies aim to reduce
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions or sequester GHG from the atmosphere. Reaching net
zero entails addressing the emissions from various economic sectors. However, as displayed
in figure 1, different sectors are on diverging emissions trajectories.1
An overarching research question emerges: Why are some sectors progressing in the
green transition and others are not? Or: what is going right in electricity (responsible for
most of the emissions categorized as “energy industries”) and road transport, and wrong
in aviation and agriculture? There is widespread consensus that the state has to and is
playing an active role in bringing about the green transition by addressing “carbon lock-
in” (Unruh 2000) or “market failure” (Boasson et al. 2022). Climate change mitigation
policies that can advance and shape the green transition have distributive consequences
and are fiercely fought over. They will serve as the central outcome of my research project,

2 Theoretical Framework
Some industries must shrink to transition modern economies to net zero, and others need
to grow (Ergen and Schmitz 2023). Therefore, Political Economy understands climate
politics as a distributive conflict between policy winners and losers (Schwander and Fis-
cher 2024). However, there is a third class of business actors with skin in the game which
most of the literature overlooks: “decarbonizable” (Kupzok and Nahm 2024) or “convert-
2
ible” (Kelsey 2018) industries must transform to transition. They rely on GHG emissions
along their supply chain, which makes them vulnerable to policy costs. However, at the
same time, they can align their business model with the green transition. Because of their
ability to change color, I call these actors chameleons: They can break their long-standing
political coalition with brown industries and join green industries in demanding specific
climate policies that help them become or stay competitive in the green transition. In
other words, while for brown industries, the green transition is “existential”, actors in
chameleon industries face “distributional” consequences (Colgan et al. 2021, p. 593).
I understand economic sectors as clusters of economic activities linked by their simi-
larity and the supply chain. Each polluting sector comprises brown, green, and chameleon
industries (their relative size can vary). Table 1 displays this classification for the elec-
tricity, road transport, aviation, and agriculture sectors.

2.1 Defining Chameleons
The green transition is a major threat to incumbent businesses because their assets might
become stranded. Assets are widely defined as any resource with economic value rang-
ing from land and raw materials over installations and cash reserves to more intangible
means like patents, know-how, workforce skills, supply chain and distribution networks,
contracts, market shares, and brands. Which assets are at risk? Existing literature has
focused on the emissions intensity of assets, often conceptualized as the amount of GHG
emissions created by value added. However, I argue that emissions intensity is only one
of two important dimensions when analyzing potentially stranded assets.
The second dimension is asset specificity. It describes how some emissions-intense
assets can be repurposed for alternative low-emissions economic activities while others are
of little value outside their current polluting application. Asset specificity can be thought
of as the degree of depreciation an existing asset experiences when it gets repurposed.
In contrast to emissions intensity, asset specificity is inherently uncertain and cannot be
accurately predicted a priori.
4
Naturally, some asset classes are less specific than others, but for a detailed evaluation,
context matters. Fossil fuel reserves are worthless if extracting it becomes impossible,
and so is a patent on spark plugs if the internal combustion engine (ICU) is phased out.
Likewise, considering a profitable application of an industrial cowshed should agricultural
emissions be eliminated is difficult. Cash reserves, on the other hand, face no deprecia-
tion whatsoever if they get funneled into different economic activities. Other assets can
be lined up between these two poles. Some hydrocarbon installations could be partly
repurposed for hydrogen and carbon capture and storage (CSS) with major depreciation.
Correspondingly, an ICU plant can be fitted with different machinery, and auto workers
can be retrained. Airports, on the other hand, already have fueling infrastructure ready
to go for sustainable aviation fuels (SAFs). Similarly, car manufacturers’ brands should
remain valuable even as the drivetrain changes.
Existing scholarship expects emissions-intense businesses to attempt to stop their
assets from becoming stranded and, hence, to oppose ambitious climate policy. I argue
that such a stance overlooks the issue of asset specificity. Each business owns a mix
of assets of varying specificity and emissions intensity with varying risks of becoming
stranded. Hence, I agree that actors holding emissions-intense and high-specificity assets
can be expected to be “brown” in their economic and political behavior. They oppose
ambitious climate policy and attempt to extract as much value as possible from their
assets as long as they still can.
An alternative strategy emerges for actors holding a majority of emissions-intense
but low-specificity assets. They have the capability of transitioning their business by
repurposing their assets. The lower the average asset specificity, the more viable this
alternative strategy becomes. The alternative strategy sharply contrasts what I have
described as “brown” behavior above. It aims to preserve the value of existing emissions-
intense assets during the business transition to low-emissions economic activities. The
long-term aim is to convert as much of the current competitive edge gained by the ac-
tor’s assets into a future competitive edge in another economic activity. For example,
a legacy car manufacturer might aim to utilize its existing brand, distribution network,
5
and supply chain to create revenues in the EV market. In the process, assets highly spe-
cific to ICU technology, like machinery, know-how, and workforce skills, might become
stranded. Politically, the strategy entails chameleons demanding specific climate policies
that safeguard their transition, alleviate risks, and promise competitive advantages.
Emissions intensity and asset specificity are continuous concepts. Nevertheless, I will
dichotomize both to create a simplified typology. I argue that brown and chameleon actors
hold emissions-intense assets at risk of becoming stranded due to the green transition.
However, brown actors hold high-specificity assets, while chameleons’ assets are of lower
specificity. Hence, chameleons can react to the green transition’s threat towards their
assets in essentially two ways – with green or brown behavior. We can classify the asset
holder as a chameleon only if the average asset specificity of emissions-intense assets is
sufficiently low, so green and brown behavior is theoretically viable.
2.2 Explaining Chameleon Preferences
Once we have established a definition of chameleons based on the availability of different
reactions to the green transition, the subsequent question is under which conditions they
choose one strategy over the other. When are chameleons brown, and when are they
green? I argue that in the initial phase of the green transition, chameleons are aligned
with brown industries and oppose the green transition, which comes with high strategic
and analytic uncertainty (Iida 1993). However, the uncertainty can be penetrated by
policy and market signals, which push chameleons to turn green (Kelsey 2018; Kupzok
and Nahm 2024).
Market signals comprise the supply of green technologies, the demand for green prod-
ucts, and the competitive threat of first-movers overtaking emerging green markets. To-
gether, demand, supply, and competition underlie chameleons’ profit expectation on green
markets.2 Furthermore, a specific green technology must dominate other alternatives to
render investments less risky. Policy signals include international agreements, net-zero
laws, regulations, industrial policies, and emissions-reduction pledges. They can initiate

investment into low-emissions technologies.

policy feedback effects and – similar to market signals – trigger a process of “asset reval-
uation” (Colgan et al. 2021), which leads to a change in chameleons’ economic interests
and political preferences. The chameleon color change is an inter-temporal trade-off of
short-term costs and risks with long-term benefits and opportunities (Jacobs 2016).
Whenever business actors publicly state their political preferences, scholars have
pointed out a “problem of preferences” (Broockman 2012; see also Broockman 2019;
Hacker and Pierson 2002). The observed statement might constitute the first-order pref-
erence, a direct translation of an actor’s economic interests independent of political en-
vironment and strategy, or it might constitute the second-order preference, a strategic
policy position not reflecting the actor’s economic interests but publicly taken to advance
them (Vormedal and Meckling 2023). Most prominently, business interests have been
shown to support the second-best policy option in climate policymaking to stop more
ambitious proposals from passing in an act of strategic accommodation (Meckling 2011).
Political signals can trigger strategic accommodation. In contrast to the color change
(which depends on the actors’ material interests being reconfigured in the process of
asset revaluation), it can be performed by green, brown, and chameleon actors. Distin-
guishing the chameleon color change from strategic accommodation is an empirical task
for which insight into preference formation will be crucial.
Chameleons’ color change from brown to green is reversible. Market and policy signals
can also increase the uncertainty of the green transition and incentivize doubling down on
polluting economic activities. However, the initial color change comes with high upfront
investments and creates green lock-in effects. Hence, only drastic signals can reverse
chameleons once turned green. I do not expect to observe chameleon reversion in the
cases I propose studying. However, reversion becomes increasingly likely as global supply
chains stall, resource prices climb, interest rates soar, and right-wing political forces
double down on emissions-intense economic practices.

2.3 Explaining Policy Outcomes
Chameleons’ color change alters the sectoral power equilibrium because they leave the
long-standing coalition with brown industries and join green industries in supporting
(specific) climate policies instead. Such a “tipping point” (Vormedal 2011) in the power
equilibrium between green and brown business actors means policymakers interested in
the green transition are confronted with a less powerful brown and a more powerful green
coalition. Hence, they are capable of enacting more ambitious policies. However, this
mechanism depends on chameleons’ (instrumental and structural) power relative to other
actors within the sector. Only if chameleons are powerful within their sector (because
they make up a big economic chunk or are well-organized and enjoy privileged access to
policymaking) can they play a pivotal role in policy battles.
Kupzok and Nahm (2024) have called the politics of chameleons turned green the
“decarbonization bargain”: Chameleons promise policymakers partial cooperation in re-
ducing emissions as long as subsidies are being paid and investments are being de-risked.
To accommodate the inclusion of agriculture, I will slightly alter the terminology to mit-
igation bargain. I expect chameleons to support any policy that safeguards and supports
their business transition and asset repurposing. A mitigation bargain is viable in sec-
tors in which green technology has the potential to send market signals sufficient for
chameleon industries to change color.
Not only does climate policy threaten assets with becoming stranded, but so do the
consequences of a changing climate. Hence, even if a mitigation bargain is not viable
in a specific sector, its chameleons might still be motivated to engage in an adaptation
bargain if their assets are climate-vulnerable. Because no first-mover advantages can be
captured in the adaptation bargain, chameleons engage in it solely motivated by loss
aversion. They are expected to support policies that bundle adaptation and mitigation
measures. Such support is only possible because their assets have a low enough specificity
to be repurposed when faced with mitigation. In contrast, brown actors might still
demand adaptation from policymakers. Their high asset specificity renders mitigation
an existential threat to them, so they lack the bargaining capacity. The existence of an
8
adaptation bargain is highly speculative.
Green chameleon industries are a necessary but, in isolation, insufficient condition
for the passage of ambitious climate policy. A second necessary condition is the will-
ingness of policymakers to advance the green transition in certain sectors. Both green
chameleons and policymakers can be considered jointly sufficient for the outcome. The
color of policymakers and chameleons is endogenous. Chameleons are more likely to turn
green when green policymakers send positive policy signals. On the other hand, policy-
makers can be pressured by chameleons to become green. That does not mean the color
between chameleons and policymakers cannot diverge. Chameleons can be brown, while
policymakers are green when the policy signals sent are too weak. On the other hand,
policymakers can be brown while chameleons are green if they are pressured by electoral
politics (Finnegan 2022) or have strong ties to brown industries (Oatley and Blyth 2024).
2.4 Alternative Explanations
I bring forward an interest group-focused account of policymaking. However, by focusing
on chameleon industries, I part ways with much of the existing interest group-focused
explanations of policy outcomes that expect any incumbent, emissions-intense business
to be automatically brown. Additionally, two alternative explanations rival the interest-
group focused account: technological determinism and electoral politics.
Technological determinism claims that politics and policy are just a reflection of tech-
nological advancements in green technologies and their price compared to brown tech-
nologies. Although technology plays a central role in my argument because it determines
chameleons’ asset revaluation, it does not determine policy outcomes in an immediate
or unpolitical way. Instead, the availability of green technologies (partly) unlocks the
political dynamics of the mitigation bargain. These advancements allow chameleons to
turn green. Chameleon support is impossible when policymakers address emissions by
increasing efficiency or reducing output. These measures regularly fail because brown
and chameleon industries stand united against policymakers’ ambitions.
The second alternative explanation is an electoral politics account in which policy-
9
makers react to public opinion by enacting policies that align with the median voter’s
preferences. As parties compete over the electorate, they position themselves strategically
in relation to the median voter and each other. Once in government, each party enacts
policies that maximize its chance of reelection. I argue that policymakers’ willingness to
advance the green transition is essential for enacting ambitious policies. However, the
realm of interest group politics constitutes an implicit veto point. A strong brown interest
group coalition can derail policymakers’ willingness. Adding to both alternative expla-
nations, I contend that interest group politics, especially the actor class of chameleons,
matter for policy outcomes.
3 Methodological Approach
I will test the plausibility of my causal argument in four process-tracing case studies on
the EU’s electricity, road transport, aviation, and agriculture sectors. The case studies
will be policy-focused and follow process-tracing methodology using publicly available
data and elite interviews. Comparing cases can increase the leverage of my argument.
However, the primary method of causal inference will be in-depth, within-case analysis
(Goertz and Mahoney 2012; Hall 2003, 2008). The four cases can illuminate my proposed
argument’s causal links and limits. If the green transition unfolds over time, electricity,
road transport, and aviation inhabit different locations on the time axis. I argue that in
electricity, chameleons have been engaged in a mitigation bargain for an extended period
while the deal has only been struck recently in road transport. In aviation, chameleons
remain opposed to the green transition. Finally, agriculture can illustrate the limits of the
mitigation bargain while being the most likely case for the emergence of an adaptation
bargain. One overarching question is whether all sectors follow a similar transition path
or if unique dynamics unfold.
My research will be policy-focused and build on the notion of politics as “organized
combat” (Hacker and Pierson 2010), in which policies constitute the prize and the terrain
of politics (Hacker and Pierson 2014). Hence, the first step of the analysis is to identify
10
important policies in the four cases. Starting from policy, analysis can work its way
forward and backward. The way forward is to identify actors that bear costs or enjoy
benefits due to the policy being enacted (or the proposal being scrapped). The way
backward is to identify actors engaged in the policy battles and categorize them into
brown, chameleon, and green industries. Notably, the results of working in both directions
can be triangulated as the theory would expect all actors with skin in the game (forward)
to participate in it (backward).
The remainder of this section not only derives observable implications and discusses
the challenges of researching EU policymaking but also introduces the current policy
regimes in the four sectors. It reflects on their specific challenges and contributions to
the overall research question, thereby justifying the case selection.
3.1 Observable Implications
In a process-tracing design, verifying the single steps of the hypothesized causal chain
is essential (Collier 2011; Waldner 2014). For every step along the way (see figure 2),
observable implications must be derived, and evidence must be collected that either
supports or undermines the proposed mechanism. Additionally, for my account to provide
added scientific value to the explanation of the outcome, it has to be observationally
distinguishable from existing alternative explanations.
My theoretical account diverges from most interest group-focused explanations of cli-
mate policy outcomes by zooming in on chameleon industries. For this concept to be
plausibly established, it has to be shown that chameleons perceive and react to the green
transition in an essentially different way from brown industries. In sectors where the
chameleon color change has happened, it is relatively straightforward to show that busi-
ness reactions to the green transition vary between the chameleon and brown industries.
However, even in sectors where chameleons remain brown, their perception of the green
transition and strategizing of possible reactions should be in stark contrast to brown in-
dustries because, even if currently not economically viable, the strategy of changing color
is theoretically on the table. Finding that asset specificity does not impact preference
11
formation would seriously undermine my theory of chameleons. Elite interviews with
executives of chameleon and brown industries can provide evidence of these preference
formation processes, usually happening behind closed doors.
My project aims not only to show that chameleons differ from brown actors but also
to inquire into their preference formation. Variation in chameleons’ policy preferences
between sectors, points in time, or firms can be leveraged to test the assumption that
policy and market signals are responsible for chameleons’ color change. This undertak-
ing should be understood as inductive, and no alternative explanations are considered.
However, the theory of chameleons would be significantly challenged if I find that asset
revaluation has not taken place and instead chameleons turning green is a consequence
of strategic accommodation, and their climate policy support an embodiment of second-
order preferences. Again, elite interviews will be invaluable because, from an outside
perspective, the chameleon color change and strategic accommodation are observation-
ally indistinguishable.
On the bigger picture, accounts based on electoral politics or technological deter-
minism can challenge an interest group-focused explanation of policy outcomes. Both
alternative explanations are in line with observing chameleons turning green. However,
they both argue that the color change is a byproduct of technological developments or
public opinion trends and does not actually serve as a meaningful link in the causal chain.
In contrast, I contend that chameleons’ color change is causally relevant as a necessary
condition for the outcome. Establishing the causal influence of chameleon preferences on
policy outcomes will be existential to showcase the added scientific value of a chameleon-
centered explanation.
The alternative explanations can be assessed in a between-case analysis. On a purely
descriptive level, it can be asked if patterns in technological advancement and public
opinion can explain the variation in policy outcomes across sectors. At first sight, they
do not. Technological advancements cannot explain why the addition of renewable capac-
ity stagnated in the 2010s (the so-called “lost decade” UNEP 2019) or why early battery
EVs were never marketized (Eisler 2022). Similarly, there seems to be a mismatch of
12
public opinion, policymaker ambition, and policy outcomes. For example, the internal
combustion engine (ICE) phase-out was implemented while being among the least popu-
lar policies among voters (Abou-Chadi et al. 2024). On the other hand, the electorate’s
preference to target private jets, its critical stance on aviation’s fuel exemptions, and
its support for measures that make short-haul train alternatives cost-competitive remain
ignored by European policymakers (Hodgson 2024). Instead, the Commission actively
pressured the Dutch government to scrap its popular plans to reduce air traffic at Am-
sterdam Schiphol airport (Sterling and Meijer 2023). Furthermore, the Commission’s
eagerness to address agricultural emissions does not correspond with public discourse
rarely connecting agriculture and climate change (Sentient Media 2023).
However, these observations are purely descriptive. More causal evidence can be
uncovered through in-depth within-case analysis. For my explanation to hold, it must
first be shown that policymakers are receptive to business preferences.3 Second, the
particular role of chameleon interests has to be established. The counter-factual what
would have happened in a specific policy battle if only chameleons had sided with the
opposing team remains hypothetical. However, actors involved in these policy battles
can share their perceptions, and their answers can be triangulated across actor types
(chameleon, brown, and green industries, policymakers, and civil society organizations).
3.2 Multi-level Policy Making in the EU
Under the von der Leyen Commission, the EU strived for global climate leadership. Its Fit
for 55 package constitutes one of the most ambitious climate policy portfolios currently in
place. High-stake policy battles occurred in various emitting sectors. Therefore, anyone
interested in ambitious climate policymaking in high-income democracies should look
towards the EU since 2019. Time is an essential dimension in the study of policy feedback
(Pierson 2004). Therefore, one may object to an observation period of five years. Three
arguments can defend my research design against this objection. First, by focusing on
a limited period characterized by the high willingness of policymakers to accelerate the

altogether. Climate policymaking can be influenced through different “channels” (Finnegan 2022).

green transition, I can focus on the role of chameleons under somewhat controlled context
conditions. Second, interviews have been shown to produce decreasingly reliable data the
further an event lies in the past. Finally, I hope to find variation usually found over time
between sectors.
Studying the EU has its pitfalls: The EU’s institutional structure presents interest
groups with several venues to influence policymaking (Bouwen 2002). I expect interest
groups to direct their resources at the Commission – especially the Directorate Generale
responsible for the legislation – as long as no proposal has been tabled. After a proposal
gets tabled, I expect interest group resources to shift (Kl¨uver 2013, p. 20). First, they
can target the Parliament – especially the Committee responsible for the proposal or
specific party groups. Second, they can target member states’ governments to influence
the Council. Swaying the position of one of the bigger economies (Germany and France)
in the EU might be a successful strategy, especially as smaller countries could follow the
influential government’s lead. Interest groups can achieve beneficial amendments or a
complete blockade of proposals via all three venues. Hence, strategic shifts of interest
group pressure between venues are likely to occur and could lead to misinterpretations of
evidence if not all three central institutional pillars of the EU are studied.
The EU’s regulatory character creates an additional pitfall. Although its fiscal ca-
pacity has been increased in reaction to the COVID-19 pandemic, its policy measures
remain very regulation-focused (Lepont and Thiemann 2024). Kupzok and Nahm (2024)
explain the recent wave of green fiscal expansion with chameleon industries’ demand
for costly policies safeguarding and de-risking their business transition. The EU’s fis-
cal capabilities are limited compared to the member states (at least the richer ones), so
chameleons might direct their fiscal policy demand at the national arena. Therefore, an
EU-exclusive analysis might risk overlooking an essential part of chameleons’ political
behavior. Chameleon industries’ influence on national policymaking will be introduced
to the analysis as suggested by the evidence.

3.3 Electricity
Electricity generation is responsible for most emissions categorized as “energy industries”
in figure 1. Electricity emissions are falling and are predicted to continue their downward
trajectory as the industry moves from coal to gas, and the share of renewable energy
production is continuously increasing. The latter trend has been fostered by policy for
decades. The beneficial role of policy feedback effects in the renewable energy transition
is well established (e.g., Meckling et al. 2015). Governments started investing in R&D in
solar and wind in the wake of the oil price crisis (Aklin and Urpelainen 2018). Feed-in
tariffs, net metering policies, and renewable portfolio standards promoted roll-out and
aided green technologies down the experience curve. As more and more electricity comes
from renewable sources, electric utilities – the most prominent chameleons in this sector
– can realign their interests to align with the green transition (Basseches 2023).

Asian legacy manufacturers, which, focusing on hybrid vehicles, missed investing early in
battery EVs.
Another sectoral puzzle emerged during the Emissions Trading System 2 policy battle
in which legacy car manufacturers supported the road transport sector to be covered by
the cap & trade. This stance is curious as the policy would increase operation costs
for ICU cars still built and sold in Europe. Additionally, the overseas premium EV
producer Tesla opposed this measure, although its cars would receive a relative decrease
in operation costs. Understanding the car industry’s policy preference on cap & trade can
illuminate the policy types chameleon industries perceive as beneficial to their business
transition.
3.5 Aviation
Aviation’s upward emissions trajectory reflects increasing output, immature green tech-
nologies, already high emissions efficiency, and little regulation. Currently, CO2-emissions
from intra-EU flights are covered by the Emissions Trading System (ETS). Besides, little
policy encourages the green transition (see table 4). On the contrary, aviation enjoys
substantial value-added and fuel tax exemptions. The UN Internation Civil Aviation Or-
ganization’s (ICAO) carbon offsetting scheme (CORSIA) is purely symbolic (European
Commission 2020a) and non-Co2-emissions (NOx, soot particles, oxidized sulfur species,
water vapor) – which have twice or thrice the impact of aviation’s CO2-emissions – are
not addressed by any policy (European Commission 2020b). Since 2012, the Commission
has unsuccessfully proposed to include international aviation emissions in the ETS. Op-

3.6 Agriculture
The agriculture sector is attractive to study in the context of climate politics for two
reasons. First, it is a major polluter. However, in contrast to energy-related pollution,
its emissions stem from biochemical processes and are tricky to measure (let alone abate).
Therefore, agriculture can illustrate the limits of the mitigation bargain. Second, agri-
culture is highly vulnerable to climate change. Hence, it is the most likely case to study
cross-pressure resulting from actors being climate change and climate policy vulnerable
(Gaikwad et al. 2022) and the emergence of an adaptation bargain.4

The EU Commission launched several legislative proposals to address the agricultural

The Nature Restoration Law (NRL) is the Commission’s only milestone when it comes
to climate legislation targeting agricultural emissions. The policy battle is known for
the unusual levels of politicization and polarization that could be observed when the
Commission’s proposal reached Parliament in the spring of 2023. The proposal did not
only divide Parliament into a supporting bloc of lefts, social democrats, greens, and
liberals versus an opposing bloc ranging from the conservatives to the far-right. There
were divisions within the agrifood supply chain, too. While farmers fiercely opposed the
proposal, the downstream food majors and retailers came out in support of it. Farming
interests found their parliamentary supporters in the EPP, eager to signal alliance with
farmers (even if it meant opposing a Commission headed by one of their own) after the
Dutch right-wing populist farmer party BBB became the strongest political force in the
2023 provincial elections (Bounds 2023).
In July 2023, the NRL survived a razor-thin plenary vote, ending a fierce and loud
policy battle with a remarkable loss for farming interests. Notably, the NRL’s passage
occurred only one day after the Parliament watered down the IED, adding to the sharp
contrast to be explained. Since its passage in Parliament, the NRL has lost its majority
support in the Council, again calling its passage into question.
The NRL mandates member states to increase the stock of organic carbon in cropland
mineral soils. Such a measure does not only increase the agricultural soil’s ability to
function as a carbon sink (mitigation). It also increases the soil’s ability to absorb and
store water and resist desertification (adaptation). Hence, the law provides long-term
adaptation benefits to the food system and imposes short-term mitigation costs. Indeed,
both distributive characteristics were used as frames during the policy battle. While
farming interests focused their political communication on the short-term mitigation costs
as the reason for their opposition to the law, food majors and retailers focused theirs on
adaptation’s long-term benefits (Niranjan 2023).
Why do the agrifood supply chain actors operate with such diverging time horizons? I
argue that for industrialized farming – and especially livestock farming –, stringent miti-
gation measures would entail moving away from intensive farming practices that currently
20
guarantee them a competitive advantage on the world market. However, food majors and
retailers would still sell food to Europeans. They might face costs as alternative supply
chains must be built, and potential price spikes might have to be absorbed by cutting
into profit margins. Nevertheless, addressing agricultural emissions means distributional
consequences to them, while it will be existential for (livestock) farmers. Threats being
merely distributive can explain food majors’ and retailers’ long-term time horizon and
willingness to engage in an adaptation bargain.
4 Contribution
My research speaks to the Political Economy of climate change literature in three distinct
ways. Until now, most scholarly work has focused exclusively on the renewable energy
transition. I will broaden the scope by investigating sectors besides energy in an attempt
to gauge the limits of the arguments made for the energy sector. Additionally, I will
focus on chameleon industries, those actors hovering between the clear-cut climate policy
winners and losers, which have not yet received the attention they deserve. This focus
also brings the question of asset specificity to the foreground. Finally, by including the
agriculture sector, I also widen current interest group approaches by introducing the
concept of climate change vulnerability into empirical studies.
Additionally, my research can potentially contribute to the wider field of Political
Economy. Researching chameleon industries can help us understand how policy and
markets can trigger radical preference shifts in businesses. Additionally, understanding
how chameleons navigate the dilemma between short-term costs and expected long-term
benefits of turning green can speak to the issue of long-term policymaking and inter-
temporal trade-offs. Finally, my research will be among the first to apply the toolkit
of Comparative Political Economy to study sectors instead of countries as the unit of
observation.
