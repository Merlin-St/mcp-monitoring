\begin{abstract}
The gradual-disempowerment scenario of \citet{kulveit2025gradual} predicts that delegated markets can decouple from human preferences as AI agents replace humans in their operation. The concept is theoretical; its \emph{precursors} -- observable, measurable patterns that would precede such a drift -- have not been systematically quantified in the wild. We offer a \textbf{first illustration} of how to do so, on a concrete ecosystem: GitHub pull-request review. We define a taxonomy of nine precursors, build an event-level detector for AI-agent activity, and illustrate the measurement of two precursors on 84,725{} pull requests from 1,000{} popular GitHub repositories (April~2025--March~2026). In this sample, AI-agent participation grew 3.5{}$\times$ in one year (from 6.5\%{} to 22.5\%{} of all PRs; contiguous AI$\to$AI event chains of length~$\geq$5 grew from 0.3\%{} to 4.8\%{}). A first within-PR test -- of the 161{} PRs where an AI bot issued an \emph{approving} review, humans co-approved 5/24 (20.8\%){} of 24{} AI-authored PRs versus 46/137 (33.6\%){} of 137{} human-authored ($z=-1.24$, two-sided $p=0.22${}) -- finds no statistically significant AI-AI bias, not yet adjusted for code quality. We release the pipeline and treat these measurements as an initial probe; the contribution is the \emph{measurability} of precursors more than any particular point estimate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

``Gradual disempowerment''~\citep{kulveit2025gradual} names a macro failure in which economic, cultural, and political systems drift from human preferences as AI increasingly replaces human labour within them. The concept is theoretical and its individual mechanisms are hard to study in isolation: they require longitudinal observation of real multi-agent systems at scale. Agent deployment is now observable at that scale~\citep{appel2025economic,stein2026mcp,shao2025workbank}, sharpening the risk of overreliance~\citep{ibrahim2025overreliance}. Prior work on \emph{AI-AI bias} has shown that LLMs prefer LLM-generated content over human content in single-turn choice experiments---GPT-4 favoured LLM-written product ads 89\% of the time vs.\ a 36\% human baseline, and LLM abstracts 78\% vs.\ 61\%~\citep{laurito2025aibias}; LLM evaluators recognise and favour their own generations~\citep{panickssery2024evaluators}. Whether analogous biases manifest \emph{in the wild}, in multi-agent systems that actually decide real-world outcomes, is an open empirical question, and so is the prior question of whether any of the gradual-disempowerment \emph{precursors} are measurable at all outside the lab.

This paper is a \textbf{first illustration} of precursor measurement in a concrete ecosystem: GitHub pull-request review. We choose GitHub because real AI coding agents act in it (Claude~Code, GitHub~Copilot coding agent, Devin, Cursor, Aider, Cline), distinct AI code-review agents (CodeRabbit, Cubic, Gemini~Code~Assist, Copilot~PR~Reviewer, Greptile) leave machine-readable traces~\citep{ghaleb2026fingerprinting,staufer2026aiagentindex}, merge outcomes are discrete, and a full year (April~2025--March~2026) covers the period in which coding-agent adoption became mainstream. Concurrent work reports high agent-PR merge rates~\citep{watanabe2025agentic,li2026aidev,li2025teammates,logicstar2025wild} alongside persistent quality debt~\citep{agarwal2026ides,ehsani2026fail,huang2026morecode}; none of it frames the measurement in disempowerment-precursor terms.

We offer three illustrative contributions. (i)~A \emph{first taxonomy} of nine precursors distinguished from the situational (within-conversation) failure patterns studied in prior work~\citep{kulveit2025gradual,sharma2026whos}. (ii)~A \emph{first open measurement pipeline} that classifies GitHub PR \emph{events} (rather than whole repositories as in \citet{ghaleb2026fingerprinting}) as human-, AI-assisted-, or AI-bot-attributed, usable as a standing monitor. (iii)~A \emph{first illustrative measurement} of two precursors on 84,725{} PRs from 1,000{} repositories, including -- to our knowledge -- the first within-PR comparison of AI-bot and human approvals on the same code; prior empirical work compares disjoint pools of PRs~\citep{zhong2026synergy,chowdhury2026claims}. Given small numerators (the key cell has only 24{} PRs), we emphasise the \emph{feasibility} of measurement; our effect-size estimates are illustrative and associational, and intended as a starting point for larger-scale tracking, not as conclusive findings.

\emph{Precursors} are observable, measurable patterns; they are not themselves disempowerment. \citet{sharma2026whos} focus on situational (within-conversation) failures; our taxonomy is ecosystem-level and is designed to be tracked from public logs over time. The table is a starting draft rather than a settled classification -- we invite extensions -- and it complements within-system MAS failure taxonomies~\citep{cemri2025mas} and autonomy-as-user-role frameworks~\citep{cihon2025autonomy,feng2025autonomylevels}.

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Repository universe.} Public GitHub repositories plausibly gaining $\geq$1{,}000 stars in 2025 and actively pushed within our analysis window, capped at the top 1,000{} by a recency-weighted activity score; not forks, not archives. Non-goal: GitHub-wide representativeness.

\paragraph{PR universe.} All PRs \emph{created} between 2025-04-01 and 2026-03-31 in those repositories (84,725{} PRs), fetched via GitHub GraphQL with \texttt{orderBy:{CREATED\_AT,DESC}}. We record metadata, commits, reviews, review-thread comments, issue comments, and merge/close timeline events.

\paragraph{Event-level AI classification.} Adapting \citet{ghaleb2026fingerprinting}'s heuristics to event granularity, we classify each actor--event pair as \textbf{AI-bot} (account on our 20-entry allowlist: \texttt{copilot-swe-agent[bot]}, \texttt{devin-ai-integration[bot]}, \texttt{cursor-agent}, \texttt{claude[bot]}, \texttt{coderabbitai}, \texttt{cubic-dev-ai}, \texttt{gemini-code-assist}, \texttt{copilot-pull-request-reviewer}, etc.\ -- see Appendix~C), \textbf{AI-assisted} (human account whose payload contains a \texttt{Co-Authored-By} trailer to an AI tool or a direct handle mention), or \textbf{human}. Confidence is \emph{high} when a bot account or co-author trailer matches, \emph{low} otherwise. Maintenance bots (Dependabot, Renovate, CI) are excluded. Primary analyses use high-confidence AI only.

\paragraph{Chain length (RQ1).} Within a PR, order attributed events by timestamp. An \emph{AI$\to$AI chain} is a maximal contiguous subsequence of events with \texttt{actor\_type=AI-bot} at high confidence. The per-PR metric is the longest such chain; we report the monthly share of PRs with chain $\geq$1, $\geq$2, $\geq$5.

\paragraph{Self-preference diff-in-diff (RQ2).} For each PR we assign \texttt{author\_type}~$\in\{$AI,human$\}$ (AI if the PR's author login is on our allowlist OR any commit bears an AI co-author trailer) and \texttt{reviewer\_type}~$\in\{$AI,human$\}$ (AI if an approving review came from our allowlist). Tie-breaking is conservative: AI wins ties, biasing \emph{against} finding AI-AI preference. Restricting to PRs with at least one approving review, the cell merge rate (within 30 days of opening) is $\mathrm{mr}[a,r]$; the diff-in-diff is
\[
    \mathrm{DiD} = (\mathrm{mr}_{AA} - \mathrm{mr}_{Ah}) - (\mathrm{mr}_{hA} - \mathrm{mr}_{hh}).
\]
Confidence intervals use a cluster bootstrap by repository (1{,}000 draws).

\paragraph{Scope: authors and reviewers, not mergers.} We measure AI participation through authoring and reviewing, not merging. No merges in our universe are attributed to AI-bot accounts on our allowlist, so ``AI merger'' is not an informative dimension in this window; we discuss the limitations of that framing---including the fact that AI can still act \emph{through} a human merger---in the Discussion.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Vertical dotted lines mark approximate public-availability dates of several coding and code-review agents (reader context; no causal claim).}
    \label{fig:headline}

\paragraph{Chain length (RQ1): participation is growing.} Figure~\ref{fig:headline} illustrates monthly agent-participation rates at three intensity levels in this sample. AI-agent participation rose from 6.5\%{} of PRs in April~2025 to 22.5\%{} in March~2026 (3.5{}$\times$). The share of PRs containing an \emph{uninterrupted} chain of at least two AI-to-AI events grew from 2.7\%{} to 16.3\%{}; chains of length $\geq$5 from 0.3\%{} to 4.8\%{}. The p95 longest-chain length doubled from 1 to 4 events. The trend is monotonic across twelve months. We read this as illustrative evidence that the \emph{chain-length} precursor is measurable and moving; a single high-activity slice of GitHub cannot establish that AI-agent activity is accelerating ecosystem-wide.

\paragraph{Self-preference (RQ2): a first look.} Table~\ref{tab:did} reports the $2\times2$ merge rates on PRs with approving reviews. The diff-in-diff is +0.2 percentage points (95\% CI -31.2 to +25.1){}; the 95\% cluster-bootstrap CI straddles zero. The table is best read as illustrative: AI code-review tools are not obviously preferentially approving AI-authored PRs, and AI-reviewed PRs in general have lower merge rates than human-reviewed ones. Only two AI bots in our data (Cubic, CodeRabbit) routinely issue approving reviews, which constrains how much weight this first contrast can carry.

\paragraph{Within-PR comparison (Fig.~\ref{fig:withinpr}).} A tighter test holds the PR (and implicitly, code quality) fixed. AI code-review tools in our sample overwhelmingly issue COMMENTED reviews rather than APPROVED ones: of 17,169{} AI-bot review events, only 172{} ($\approx$1.0\%{}) are approvals, and these come almost entirely from Cubic~AI (n=85) and CodeRabbit (n=19); Copilot~PR~Reviewer, Gemini~Code~Assist, and Greptile post critiques but almost never approve. The rejection channel is equally thin (AI bots issued \texttt{changes\_requested} on only 11 PRs). Using ``AI reviewer'' strictly to mean ``AI bot that issued an APPROVED review'', we ask whether a human reviewer and an AI approver, looking at the same PR, agree more readily when the PR is AI-authored. Of the 161{} PRs where an AI bot approved, 24{} (14.9\%{}) were AI-authored; a human co-approved 5/24 (20.8\%){} of those, versus 46/137 (33.6\%){} of the 137{} human-authored AI-approved PRs. The point-estimate gap (+12.7{}~pp) is directionally consistent with a small AI-AI bias, but $z=-1.24$, two-sided $p=0.22${} and the 95\% CIs overlap substantially. \textbf{With a key cell of only 24{} PRs, we treat this as an illustration of the test, not a finding}: we demonstrate that a within-PR AI-AI-bias measurement is feasible on public GitHub data, and report the first such contrast. A larger approving-agent population is required before the direction can be read as a substantive claim.

\section{Discussion and call to action}
\label{sec:discuss}

\paragraph{What the illustration shows.} As a demonstration of measurability, three things hold in this sample. Chain length is easy to track and is moving in the direction predicted by disempowerment concerns. The cross-cell DiD is estimable but structurally under-powered because so few AI bots issue approving reviews. The within-PR contrast is the tightest test currently feasible on public GitHub data; with 24{} AI-authored, AI-approved PRs it is under-powered for an effect size at the single-digit-pp scale. A larger population of approving AI agents --- or a longer window --- would convert this illustration into a test with meaningful power. AI-mediated peer review~\citep{thakkar2025iclr} and social sycophancy~\citep{cheng2025elephant} suggest that AI can shape human judgments without overt preference, consistent with overreliance risk~\citep{ibrahim2025anthropomorphic}; our measurement apparatus would let such effects, if they emerge, be tracked over time rather than discovered retrospectively.

\paragraph{Call to action.} Treat precursor tracking as a first-order empirical task rather than a by-product of capability evaluation. If this pipeline can be built from public logs in a few hundred lines of Python, similar measurements should become standing dashboards: platforms (GitHub, Stack Overflow, npm, package registries) should publish monthly AI-agent activity reports; model providers should include cross-family self-preference in post-training evaluations; sectoral regulators should require the same for regulated agent markets (finance, healthcare). The raw signal is already in public logs; this paper is a first illustration that it can be extracted and tracked.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We flag seven main limitations; full discussion in Appendix~B.
(i)~\emph{Selection.} Our 500 repositories are the top of a high-activity slice of GitHub (median stars 53{,}583; see Appendix~E for the exact pipeline). We do not claim representativeness of GitHub-wide PR behaviour.
(ii)~\emph{Detection false-negatives.} Event-level heuristic detection cannot recover deliberately unattributed agent activity. Commercial coding agents reportedly include modes that suppress attribution in outward artefacts; the headline growth figure is therefore a lower bound.
(iii)~\emph{Detection false-positives.} Our 20-entry bot allowlist (Appendix~C) may include accounts that are AI-\emph{assisted} but not AI-\emph{agent} in the sense defined in §\ref{sec:intro}. We accept this trade-off because false positives move PRs from `human' into `AI', \emph{reducing} the measured author-$\times$-reviewer contrast and biasing \emph{against} our null finding.
(iv)~\emph{Thin AI$\times$AI cell for RQ2.} Only two bots in our data (Cubic AI, CodeRabbit) routinely post \emph{approving} reviews; the AI-author $\times$ AI-reviewer cell holds only 24{} approved PRs even across 500 repos and twelve months. The DiD CI is therefore wide by construction, not by chance.
(v)~\emph{Confounding by tool announcements.} Several coding- and review-agent GAs happened within our window (vertical lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:headline}). We make no causal attribution; the chain-length trend is associational.
(vi)~\emph{Cluster-bootstrap assumptions.} Resampling repositories assumes exchangeability conditional on the $2\times 2$ cell; a few very active repositories contribute disproportionately.
(vii)~\emph{Merger attribution.} We focus on AI \emph{reviews}, not AI \emph{merges}. No merges in our universe are attributed to AI-bot accounts on our allowlist, so direct ``AI merger'' activity is currently zero. A human still clicks the merge button, but AI may still act \emph{through} that human---e.g., when an AI review's ``approved'' state is what gates the maintainer's decision. Our design cannot distinguish a maintainer who merged \emph{because of} an AI approval from one who merged in spite of it; a fuller causal account of merge gating is future work.
We make no causal claims in either direction.
